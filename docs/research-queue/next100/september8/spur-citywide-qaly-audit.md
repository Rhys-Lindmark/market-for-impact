# SPUR housing portfolio: citywide health audit

September 8, 2026. Primary-source tracing of [The housing theory of everything](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-housing-theory-of-everything/), John Myers, Sam Bowman and Ben Southwood, September 14, 2021. Research asset, not a new SPUR funding offer or complete cost-effectiveness model.

## Bottom line

**A citywide model is legitimate and potentially important. Restricting housing benefits to occupants of new units would miss rent, displacement and transport spillovers. But city population is an exposure denominator, not the treatment effect.** A defensible model must predict the marginal change in outcomes across existing residents, incoming households, displaced households and their counterfactual locations. Neither the full benefit of solving the housing shortage nor every healthy year of everyone in SF belongs to a marginal SPUR gift.

The essay connects restricted housing supply to affordability, productivity, innovation, family formation, urban form and emissions. It is a mechanism-generating synthesis, not causal evidence that a particular nonprofit changes population health. Its cross-country obesity/car-use comparisons should not become a direct density-to-QALY coefficient. The strongest implementable health extension is a spatial housing/transport exposure model; the weakest is multiplying income, patents or desired births by an assumed health conversion.

Actual current institutional fit: [SPUR, Permitting Progress, May 2026 source file](https://www.spur.org/sites/default/files/2026-05/SPUR_Permitting_Progress.pdf), especially printed page 15, documents fragmented SF planning/building processes and proposals for coordinated permitting. This verifies relevant research activity, not policy adoption, attributable housing completions or donor leverage. Future implementation assistance is a plausible non-electoral scope. Existing PermitSF activity and already-adopted rules are baseline. This broader housing analysis is not the same intervention as SPUR's previously modeled appliance/clean-heat work.

## Primary evidence map

### Supply → rent effects beyond direct occupants

[Asquith, Mast and Reed, working paper 2020](https://research.upjohn.org/up_workingpapers/316/), [published March 2023](https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article/105/2/359/100977/Local-Effects-of-Large-New-Apartment-Buildings-in): large market-rate apartment construction in low-income areas reduced nearby rents by 5–7% relative to slightly farther or later-developed comparisons, with more in-migration from low-income areas. This is a local quasi-experimental comparison and supports spillovers. It is **not** a citywide 5–7% decrease per building, a permanent annual compound decrease or a percentage-point reduction in homelessness. Overlapping project catchments cannot each receive the same full local effect. SF transfer requires actual project scale, location, rent regulation and vacancy conditions.

[Pennington, June 15, 2021 SF working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3867764), [primary manuscript copy](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Pennington%20-%20Does%20Building%20New%20Housing%20Cause%20Displacement%20The%20.pdf), uses construction associated with building fires to address endogenous location. The paper reports a 17% reduction in renters' risk of moving to a lower-income neighborhood near new construction. **This is relative risk and a specific displacement proxy**, not 17% of all SF residents kept housed, not all moves prevented and not measured avoided homelessness. The fire/redevelopment identification strategy also merits scrutiny for direct fire and reconstruction effects before a precise coefficient is transferred. Primary indexed manuscript was inspected; the SSRN landing page later failed retrieval.

### Incumbent protection and supply can point in different directions

[Diamond, McQuade and Qian, AER September 2019](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20181289) exploits SF's 1994 rent-control expansion. It finds less incumbent mobility/displacement while treated landlords reduced rental supply by 15%, including sales to owner-occupants and redevelopment. The treatment is rent-control expansion, not a generic supply reform. Its [working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181) includes a modeled 5.1% citywide rent increase: distinguish this equilibrium calculation from directly estimated household outcomes. Do not mechanically reverse the law's effect to forecast any SPUR proposal. Include losses to specifically displaced/incumbent households even if aggregate supply benefits are positive.

### Housing/neighborhoods → measured health: relevant but not fungible

[Ludwig et al., NEJM October 20, 2011](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa1103216): 4,498 women with children in high-poverty public housing were randomized among low-poverty vouchers plus counseling, ordinary vouchers and control. At 2008–2010 follow-up, low-poverty assignment reduced prevalence of BMI≥35 by 4.61 percentage points and elevated HbA1c by 4.31 points relative to control. These are long-term intention-to-treat endpoint differences after a substantial neighborhood intervention, not annual utility gains or effects of a small rent reduction. Severe-obesity and diabetes outcomes overlap; a joint disease model is needed. A $1 market-rent reduction cannot inherit a voucher relocation effect. Adult women are not all residents; a child benefit cannot be inferred by multiplying the adult coefficient by children.

Rent stress, eviction, crowding, indoor hazards and mental health are plausible additional channels, but no source in this bounded tracing establishes a universal marginal rent-to-QALY coefficient. Estimate actual transitions in these states first; use program-matched causal health evidence or clearly judgmental bridges. Stable housing versus homelessness utility differences cannot be assigned to every renter who saves money.

### Density/location → activity: stronger new evidence, still not QALYs

The essay's [Althoff et al., Nature July 10, 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature23018) used 68 million smartphone-days from 717,527 people and associated walkability/activity inequality with obesity. Selection and cross-sectional geography prevent reading this as a housing-policy treatment effect.

A useful newer primary study is [Althoff et al., Nature, online August 13 / issue September 11, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09321-3): 5,424 people made 7,447 relocations among 1,609 US cities. Large walkability increases were associated with about 1,100 additional daily steps, sustained for three months, with symmetry and similar-walkability comparisons addressing selection. This supports a behavior bridge more strongly than cross-country comparisons. It does not prove decades of persistence, disease reductions or an equivalent response among existing SF residents who do not move. SF moves appear in extended analyses; an already walkable SF origin can leave little marginal gain. A new building alone does not imply a 49-point Walk Score increase.

### Travel/pollution → health: citywide machinery exists

[Maizlish et al., AJPH April 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3673232/), DOI 10.2105/AJPH.2012.300939, models Bay Area scenarios with physical activity, traffic injuries and PM2.5. One active-transport scenario projects 2,404 avoided premature deaths and 44,866 DALYs annually across the region. Those are **modeled full-scenario regional outcomes**, not measured changes, SF-only benefits or SPUR effects. DALYs are not automatically identical to QALYs. Use its pathway architecture or rerun cause-specific life tables with current local inputs; do not award the entire result and multiply by a casual advocacy share. Transport emissions and actual concentrations differ. New walking/cycling can increase exposure/injury unless infrastructure improves; motor-vehicle miles and population may change in opposite directions.

[Glaeser and Kahn, Journal of Urban Economics May 2010](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~jkbrueck/course%20readings/glaeser-kahn.pdf) estimates household carbon footprints across cities and city/suburb locations. SF's climate and electricity context matter in addition to density. These are historical modeled emissions, not a current marginal household relocation experiment. Lower CO2 is not lower local PM2.5 one-for-one, and carbon tons require a separate global damage model—not an arbitrary QALY conversion.

### Large economic benefits: retain outside health denominator

[Hsieh and Moretti, AEJ Macroeconomics April 2019](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20170388) uses a spatial equilibrium model and 220 metropolitan areas; its abstract estimates housing constraints lowered aggregate US growth by 36% over 1964–2009. The essay's GDP counterfactual is not an estimate of a marginal current SF reform. Growth over a historical interval, income levels and annually repeated gains are different units. Agglomeration, earnings, innovation, wealth redistribution and desired family formation can be valuable without being QALYs. Do not use a generic income-health gradient causally, award all future innovations, or count hypothetical additional births as conventional patient-health gains. Report these as separate welfare/economic outcomes.

## Inspectable whole-city model structure

Use one specified future reform or bounded implementation portfolio, a dated baseline and a fixed grant budget—not 'housing reform generally.' Two valid attribution methods must not be combined:

1. Direct: estimate housing and exposure trajectories under gift versus no gift. No further advocacy/additionality multiplier.
2. Conditional: model exposure if a defined policy improvement occurs, then multiply by the **incremental probability/contribution attributable to the new gift**, with funding substitution separately only if not already included. Partner contributions must share one policy ledger.

For the conditional method:

`E[Qgift] = attribution × Σ(year t, population cell g) [N(g,t) × {S1(g,t)u1(g,t) − S0(g,t)u0(g,t)} / (1+r)^t] − independent donor harms`

Cells cover baseline residents, incoming residents and displaced people, with age, health and geography. Use matched origin/destination counterfactuals; otherwise migration alone can change city average health without improving anyone's health. Report SF, rest-of-Bay and outside-region effects separately. An entire city's exposure is allowed where rents, ambient pollution or travel actually change; a uniform treatment effect is not presumed.

Intermediate model: additional completed/occupied net homes over time → rent/vacancy and migration changes → person-years of reduced housing stress/crowding, changed neighborhood/activity and changed travel → disease/utility trajectories. Include financing/construction feasibility: an entitlement or permit is not an occupied home. Acceleration stops at comparator catch-up; permanent net additions need a separately justified persistence horizon. Existing residents' rents can change without relocation; that pathway must not be omitted merely because they receive no unit.

**All current SPUR attribution, new completion leverage, duration and unspecified health bridges are judgments.** Literature can calibrate individual arrows, not supply the entire causal product. Prefer a joint microsimulation or nonoverlapping state model. An independent channel sum is only a labeled approximation and must be tested against overlapping morbidity.

## Double-counting protections and falsifiers

- Do not add BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality as independent full benefits from activity. Use a common competing-risk life table.
- Do not add an observed net housing utility gain to separate mental-health/safety/activity gains already contained in it.
- Keep CO2/global climate, local PM2.5 and appliance emissions separate; reconcile overlap with the existing SPUR clean-heat model.
- Lower rents are partly transfers from owners; affordability value is not itself a health gain. Construction spending and public implementation are resources; financing flows are not automatically resources. Report donor and full-resource perspectives separately.
- Count moving chains as allocation mechanisms, not one full new-home health gain for every move. Apply population benefits to the actually changed exposure, not all residents times a per-treated-person effect.
- Include construction noise/dust, demolition/displacement, crash risks, crowded transit and delayed safety inspections where relevant. Faster permitting must not mean waived safety.
- Reject a high-yield case if it needs the full regional transport scenario, permanent 1,100-step increases for everyone, universal housing-state improvement or credit for existing law. New evidence that projects remain financially infeasible also breaks the chain before health.

Threshold discipline: a $100,000 marginal research/implementation grant needs **more than 10 attributable incremental QALYs** to beat $100,000 per 10Q. If an independently modeled policy produces 10,000 QALYs, a 0.1% gift contribution only reaches equality; neither 10,000 nor 0.1% is established here. This illustrates why small real citywide effects can matter, while arbitrary population multiplication cannot establish cost-effectiveness.

## Recommended next bounded empirical task

Choose one actual future SPUR-supported permitting implementation change; obtain its affected project pipeline, realistic incremental completion/acceleration and funding scope. Build a geocoded population exposure forecast jointly with transport/housing analysts. Start health calculation with one well-defined pathway, retain citywide spillovers, and add others only through the same population/health ledger. Publish broad economic upside separately rather than quietly converting it to health. No new central SPUR portfolio estimate is asserted by this audit.
