# SPUR as a whole organization: portfolio evidence and modeling boundaries

September 8, 2026. Research-only primary-source asset; no Site changes, outreach or new organization count. This broadens the existing SPUR review rather than adding another charity. It does not extrapolate the clean-heat estimate to SPUR's whole portfolio.

## What the whole organization actually is

SPUR—San Francisco Bay Area Planning and Urban Research Association, EIN 94-1498232—combines research, education and advocacy. Its [2024 Form 990, Part III, physical page 3](https://www.spur.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/SPUR_2024_Form_990.pdf), covering April 1, 2024–March 31, 2025, groups six areas into ONE $4,319,540 policy-program total: planning, housing, sustainability/resilience, transportation, governance and economy. It does not publish six corresponding spending amounts. The return lists $6,077,824 total expenses, distinct from the audit's $6,047,824; do not interchange these bases or infer a topic split. The $30,000 difference needs reconciliation before presenting reconciled cross-document totals.

The [financial-disclosure index](https://www.spur.org/about/financial-statements), read September 8, identifies these as its latest listed audited/990 documents and explains the noncalendar reporting period. Official identity is not a claim that every general gift supports SF-only work.

## Portfolio map: actual work and prospective health mechanisms

All pathways below are analyst proposals for evaluating documented work, **not measured SPUR health effects or verified purchasable marginal projects**. A report recommendation, passed rule or public financing amount is not itself a causal health outcome.

### 1. Housing and land use

The [current housing page](https://www.spur.org/policy-area/housing) is the appropriate portfolio entry point; the accepted annual-report material documents SF zoning and regional/state housing work. A particularly concrete SF implementation lead is [Reinventing Downtown, February 4, 2026](https://www.spur.org/publications/policy-brief/2026-02-04/reinventing-downtown): a proposed authority coordinating capital projects, real estate/public financing, small-business support and workforce housing. The brief calls for investigating legal structure, approvals and an investment plan; it is not evidence that the authority or its housing has been delivered.

Prospective chain: additional technical work → genuinely earlier feasible projects versus existing city/developer/advocate effort → net occupied housing and reduced harmful instability → finite resident health gains. Required: completed SF units and timing, baseline project viability, displacement/vacancy, affected populations, net health utility and substantial implementation/construction costs. Zoning capacity and office-conversion possibilities cannot substitute for completed homes. Housing/transport/climate benefits of the same infill project require a joint ledger.

### 2. Transportation and street safety

[Taking Muni’s Vitals, March 26, 2026](https://www.spur.org/publications/research/2026-03-26/taking-munis-vitals) compares agency efficiency, productivity and effectiveness with peers and identifies further efficiency work. Its deficit projections are public-agency financing needs, not SPUR funding needs. This offers a non-electoral analysis/implementation pathway distinct from the organization's separately documented electoral advocacy.

Prospective chain: better operations or genuinely preserved useful service → changed actual travel/access → fewer crash injuries, improved healthcare access or activity-related health. Required: the gift's causal service-hours change, rider mode substitution, injury exposure and absolute risks, service quality, fare/access harms and public operating costs. Neither dollars raised for transit nor all transit riders are incremental QALYs. Use mutually consistent pollution/activity/injury endpoints; fiscal efficiency alone is not health improvement.

### 3. Economy and household financial security

[Getting In on the Ground Floor, December 10, 2025](https://www.spur.org/publications/policy-brief/2025-12-10/getting-ground-floor) proposes five activation strategies and 13 recommendations for **downtown San José**, including leasing, public-realm and arts uses, with a one-to-two-year implementation orientation. Its “feels safer” mechanism is not measured violent injury reduction, and SF outcomes cannot be assigned to San José activity.

The [Economic Justice portfolio page](https://www.spur.org/policy-area/economic-justice) describes safety-net access, progressive fiscal policy, fines/fees and cash-policy research. Located updates are substantially older (2023–24); their presence does not establish an active 2026 delivery budget. The next question is whether staff are implementing a specific present benefit-access reform—not whether a past report favored cash transfers. Prospective health chain: actual additional net benefits received by otherwise excluded households → reduced material hardship → measured short health utility. No income-to-QALY conversion or historical food-incentive credit is justified automatically.

### 4. Governance, procurement and public services

[Purchasing Power, April 17, 2025](https://www.spur.org/publications/spur-report/2025-04-17/purchasing-power) gives nine recommendations for SF procurement, describing long contracting times (8.5 months–1.5 years). This is a plausible non-electoral implementation opportunity: help a defined reform reliably bring effective services online earlier. It is not justified to multiply an assumed percentage of the whole city budget by a health ratio.

Prospective chain: additional implementation/evaluation → net fewer service-delay days or fewer failed procurements → extra delivery of a specifically effective health service → outcome-specific QALYs. Need recipient service, true bottleneck, comparator reform schedule, staffing/contract costs and attribution alongside city staff. Faster contracting can worsen screening or quality; cheaper contracts need not improve health. General governance competence remains valuable but unpriced until an identifiable service pathway exists.

### 5. Climate, environmental health and physical resilience

[Shared Risk, Shared Resilience, January 7, 2026](https://www.spur.org/publications/spur-report/2026-01-07/shared-risk-shared-resilience) examines Bay Area wildfire coordination, long-term funding and insurance alignment, discussing Marin/East Bay governance models. Prospective implementation could reduce exposure or improve mitigation; insured property value is not QALYs. Required: changed mitigation actually attributable to additional SPUR work, location-specific burn/smoke risk, health impacts, maintenance and costs. SF residents can experience regional smoke even where treatment is outside SF, but that exposure pathway must be modeled rather than inferred from an address.

[Greenlighting Clean Heat, June 6, 2025](https://www.spur.org/publications/policy-brief/2025-06-06/greenlighting-clean-heat) remains one real component, already separately reviewed. Its health model must remain a component—not SPUR's organization-wide effectiveness. Procurement/housing improvements that enable the same heat conversions must not each claim the full benefit. Seismic, flooding and heat resilience belong in the broader portfolio search, but this bounded asset supplies no additional causal coefficients for them.

## What a defensible whole-organization estimate would require

Use one SPUR organization with multiple mechanism components. For an unrestricted gift G, document marginal allocation weights w_i, not six equal historical shares or the reported 71% program ratio. Model Q(G) = sum of net, mutually deduplicated health changes caused by G through each component, less donor-specific harms. Topic-level Q depends on additional staff capacity, implementation probabilities, collaborator contribution, comparator catch-up, geography, finite health duration and induced public/private resource costs. These are not resolved by a general donation page.

A low-confidence **conditional portfolio scenario** may assign explicit judgmental weights and causal priors, but must label them as such and test alternate allocations, displacement and zero effect. It should not be called an empirically measured whole-SPUR estimate. Retain unmodeled welfare/civic benefits as unpriced, not necessarily absent. Past achievements are evidence of capability, not benefits bought again by the next dollar. Include fundraising/admin once in the funded budget, not as an additional arbitrary effectiveness penalty.

Highest-value next allocation questions: restricted versus flexible funding; planned staffing by topic/city; which additional task a gift enables or accelerates; donor-funded work that would otherwise lapse; topic implementation budgets; and overlap with public agencies and other advocates. None was verified as a current priced offer here.
